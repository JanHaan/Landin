# Document ownership and checks

`tour.md` explains the language and `spec.md` decides it. This says how each
is laid out, so that a reader looking for a rule knows where to look and an
editor adding one knows where to put it. It is derived from the documents
and is not an authority over them. `[1840]` names a numbered paragraph of
`tour.md` or `spec.md`, and `D148` a decision in `spec.md`'s register;
`refine explain` prints the title of either.

## Both are arranged by subject

Neither document is in the order its contents were written. `tour.md`
teaches in subject sections, from comments through to the toolchain, and
`spec.md` follows the same subjects in the same order, so a question about
arrays is answered in one place whichever document it is asked of.

`spec.md` is three parts:

| part | what it holds |
| --- | --- |
| the grammar of the enabled kernel, [1740] to [1830] | what the compiler accepts today, and nothing else. It shrinks as the language grows. |
| the rules the tour left unsaid, [1840] onward | the permanent rules a tutorial omits because a reader supplies them. Grouped: names, scopes and doc comments, types and literal context, what an operator takes, places and assignment, calls and errors, module values, and the boundaries the compiler owns. |
| the recorded decisions | subject sections, each decision with what the tour said before, what was chosen, the alternative, and the fixture that pins it. |

A construct id is a stable citation anchor and never moves. The consequence
is that the numbers no longer ascend down the page: [1950] sits beside
[1890] because both say what an operator takes, and D1 sits beside D233
because both say what a declaration introduces. Within a register section,
too, related decisions sit together whatever their numbers.
Nothing that cites an id has to know where it sits.

## Where a new rule goes

A rule about the shape of accepted source belongs in the grammar, and
`check_grammar_corpus` will hold it to the fixture corpus. The structural
editor grammar in `highlight/tree-sitter` transcribes the same shape, and the
gate's `editor-grammar` job parses the corpus with it; `check.py` does not, so
run `./highlight/test.sh --integration` inside `nix develop` after a grammar
or positive fixture change, which `python3 scripts/activations.py` names
when the changes reach it. A change to the editor grammar or its queries
takes two commits: the editors fetch the grammar by commit, so after the
commit that changes it, `python3 highlight/grammar_pin.py` moves their pins
to that commit, and its rewrite is committed on its own
([`highlight/README.md`](../highlight/README.md#installing-editor-and-ide-support)).
Until then `test.sh` passes every other check and fails on the pins. A rule about what
an accepted program means belongs in [1840] onward, in the group whose
subject it shares. If the rule was a choice rather than a transcription of
something `tour.md` already said, it also gets a register entry, in the
register section matching its subject — and that entry names the fixture
that pins it, which `check.py` requires to exist.

A `**Pinned by**` or `**Evidence**` paragraph names live fixtures only, and
`check.py` refuses one naming a fixture that is not in the tree, whatever
tense the sentence is in. When a fixture a decision cites is retired, the
paragraph is reworded to name what pins the decision now, and the retired
fixture, if its history matters, is named in a paragraph of its own after
it: the check reads a pinning paragraph up to its blank line and no
further.

A new construct, a `### [NNNN]` in either document, must also appear in four
places before `check.py` accepts the tree: a row in the construct inventory of
[`compiler/tests/registers.md`](../compiler/tests/registers.md#construct-inventory);
fixtures whose `constructs:` metadata claims it when the inventory calls it
`executed` or `compiled`, and none when it is `deferred`, `transferred` or
`advisory`; for an implemented construct, at least one guarantee row of
D148's register in `spec.md`, without which `check.py` reports
"implemented construct [NNNN] has no guarantee row"; and
`compiler/tests/constructs.matrix`, regenerated by
`python3 check.py --generate`. Its id is one no document uses, placed
between its neighbours by subject: ids step by ten, and where the tens
between two neighbours are all taken, as they are up to [2000], it takes a
unit id between them, as [1795] and [1975] did.

The subject is decided by what the rule decides, not by its heading. Two
register entries were filed under the wrong subject on a first pass for
exactly that reason: D154 reads as a function-outcome rule and is a
`core/diag` rule, and D185 reads as a scope rule and is the condition-binding
form.

## What the reorganisation preserved, and how that is known

The reorganisation moved units; it did not rewrite them. All 443 units — 270
in `spec.md`, 173 in `tour.md` — moved with their content unchanged, checked
by parsing both documents before and after and comparing every unit by its
key. The prose that was edited is the prose that the moves made false: the
two documents' own descriptions of how they are ordered.

`check.py`'s grammar corpus is the standing evidence that the language did
not change with the documents. It derives the positive fixture corpus from
`spec.md`'s own grammar while the Ada parser meets the same corpus from the
other side, so a grammar broken while being moved fails there rather than in
a review.

That covers the syntax, and it is worth being exact about what it leaves
uncovered. Nothing in this repository checks that a prose rule from [1840]
on, or a register entry, still means what it meant: a rule whose meaning
changed while it was being reworded would pass every check here. They are
trusted because the rework that arranged them moved them rather than
rewriting them, and nothing re-checks that. An editor who rewords one has
only the fixture a register entry names, the nearest thing to a check that
the entry still describes the compiler.

## Mechanical document checks

The named-file check resolves repository file references in live documents
and Ada comments. External URLs, including their queries and fragments,
are outside that local-file check; local references beside them still have
to resolve. Network link validation belongs to the link workflow. A fenced
listing, whether a program, its output, a grammar or JSON, is exempt: a
file it names is one the example opens or writes, and needs no other
ending than the one it would have. A command fence (`sh`, `shell`, `bash`,
`console`) is read for names with a directory, which name a repository
script or file, and not for a bare file name, which is an operand the
example invents. Prose and code spans outside a fence are checked whatever
they name, so prose spells a file an example invents with an ending the
check does not read. A path a proposal adds before it exists is written
with the word `proposed` directly before it, as in "the proposed
`scripts/flash_board.py`", and the check then accepts that path anywhere in
the same document, its command fences included; once the path exists the
check refuses the word, so the proposal is reworded in the change that
adds it.

Three rules follow from how the checks themselves are tested and fed.
Every `DERIVATION.md` under `compiler/tests` is a live document by its
name, held to the same checks as the pages listed in `check.py`'s
`LIVE_DOCS`; a new derivation's map needs no entry there. A published
example whose routine other fixtures carry a copy of (`SHARED_ROUTINES`)
is the source of each copy: after editing the routine, `python3
scripts/sync_routines.py` writes it into the copies, and the refusal of a
differing copy names that command. And the controls in `scripts/tests`
that show a check fires copy a real document and change one place in it,
which they find by the document's own words; those words are listed in
`scripts/tests/control_quotes.json`, and `python3 check.py` reports a
document that no longer says them, naming the list, so a rewording that
reaches a control is seen before the gate's `scripts` job. Reword the
quote in the list and in the control with the document. A quote a control
assembles at run time is not listed, so `python3 scripts/script_tests.py
test_check_controls` remains the full answer after rewording a page.

`check.py` also checks the grammar in `spec.md`: it reads the productions, holds every rule to being defined and reachable, and derives every `.ldn` under `compiler/tests/fixtures/positive`. A negative fixture is held to being *underivable* only when the frontend is what refuses it: one a later stage refuses is legal source and must derive, which its `codes:` is what says (see below). A grammar change that breaks a fixture, or a fixture the grammar cannot derive, fails there. `check.py` does not parse the corpus with the structural editor grammar in `highlight/tree-sitter`; the gate's `editor-grammar` job does, and `./highlight/test.sh --integration` runs that pass inside `nix develop`, which `python3 scripts/activations.py` names whenever the changes reach the grammar, a positive, runtime or ABI fixture or `core/`. A change to the editor grammar or its queries takes two commits, the second moving the editors' pins with `python3 highlight/grammar_pin.py` (`highlight/README.md`). Do not weaken a fixture to make a grammar change pass — the corpus is the agreement the parser has to meet, and the parser suite requires the same verdict from the other side.

A negative fixture's `codes:` is an ordered list, and it also says which stage refused the fixture: `check.py` reads which codes the frontend raises out of the two packages that raise them and requires the grammar to derive a program that only a later stage refused. Do not read a stage off a code's number — the catalogue's header forbids it, and `L0010` began in lexical refusal and is now raised only by the parser.

Four tables in the compiler are transcriptions of the grammar rather than paraphrases of it, and `check.py` compares each with its source. `Landin.Tokens`' reserved words must be `spec.md`'s own `keyword` production. `Landin.Syntax.Precedence` must have [1820]'s levels in [1820]'s order, with the same operators at each, the same fold, the same prefix set and first sets that agree with the grammar's own. The parser's refusal tables cover the words [1760] does not reserve, so only the parser can meet them; the checker's refused-type table covers scalar type names the grammar admits but the kernel has deferred. Both tables must spell words the tour writes, cite paragraphs that exist, and state each refused form's standing: a recorded source-form boundary, a withdrawn form, or a transfer to a successor roadmap. `Landin.Types` spells the thirteen enabled scalar names separately because it maps each onto a machine width. Add a level, an operator, a refused construct or a type in one place and the check says which other place disagrees.

The five documents are Markdown, and the form carries invariants rather than
being a style. A construct is `### [NNNN] Title` at column 0 and nothing else
is; a citation is inline and can never be mistaken for a definition, which in
the `.txt` form it could — 33 lines looked like definitions and were
citations, and 16 real definitions sat indented inside one example, which is
how [1050] was missed twice. A Landin example is a fenced block tagged
`landin`, a production is tagged `landin-grammar`, and an untagged fence is a
fault: what a block *is* is stated rather than guessed, and the heuristic it
replaced accepted 114 lines of English as code. Prose is never indented four
spaces, because Markdown reads that as a code block — an aligned list belongs
in a table.

The full run holds the library's sources to three more things. Through
the reference pages' collector, `docs/site/library.py`, every public
declaration has its `---` documentation and every module an overview in
`docs/library.md` that links an executable example. Every `core` module is
known in the places `core/README.md` lists for a new one. And every public
routine and value of `core/`, `hosted/` and `platform/` has a consumer
outside them, directly or through library routines a consumer reaches;
`core/README.md`'s "Adding a facility" says what to do when the check
refuses one. That check is a bounded reader rather than the compiler's
resolver: it blanks comments and literals and follows imports and a
module's own bare names, so a local that shares a routine's name can hide a
missing consumer but never invent one. Its two lists in `check.py` are the
routines the compiler starts and the generated tables.

`refine explain` prints a paragraph of `tour.md` or `spec.md`, or a
decision, from `citations.txt`, a data file `python3 check.py --generate`
writes at the top of the checkout and the compiler reads from its library
at run time (D1240). So an edit to the prose of either document, a
reworded sentence included, needs that run, which rewrites the entry's
line; a changed title also rewrites `compiler/tests/constructs.matrix`.
Until it is run the full `python3 check.py` fails with "the citations are
stale". The regenerated file is committed with the edit. No compiler
source changes, so nothing is rebuilt: the cases that read the file,
`driver/explain answers citations` and `commands/help and queries`, read
the new one on their next run. `citations.txt` is never edited or merged
by hand; `.gitattributes` merges it by union, and the run after a merge
puts it right.

A diagnostic code's explanation is the other kind: `docs/diagnostics.md`
is compiled into `refine` as `landin-diagnostics-explanations.adb`, so an
edit to that page does need `python3 check.py --catalogue` and a rebuild.

`R§n` and `H§n` citations preserved in the roadmap refer to an external design archive; the tracked repository does not depend on that archive.

The documents selected in `docs/site/render_html.py` are published as reading
copies described in the [site notes](site/README.md). The text files are
the sources; the pages are
generated and never edited by hand. See `docs/site/README.md` for the scope.

The mark lives in `assets/`, not in the site renderer. `assets/icon.svg` is
the drawing — `701` as a path, so no renderer needs Futura — and
`assets/landin_icon.py` is every rendering of it: the light, dark, contrast,
`currentColor`, monochrome and `prefers-color-scheme` variants, the inline
fragment, and the `data:` URL a page carries. Its four colours are the
site's own, and `check.py` holds them to the stylesheet in
`docs/site/render_html.py` and to the drawing's own attributes. Add a
variant there rather than in a consumer, and keep it standard-library-only
so the site keeps its no-dependency build. See `assets/README.md`.

The two faces the pages are set in are declared in `assets/fonts.py`, not
in the site renderer. Each family is the subset webfont package its source
delivers, and `assets/fonts.py` is every rendering of them: the
`@font-face` block a page carries, the `--ui` and `--mono` stacks, and the
list of files copied beside the pages. `Nunito Sans` is under the OFL and
vendored in `assets/fonts/`; `MonoLisaCode` is under a foundry EULA that
forbids passing the files on, so it lives in the private `landin-fonts`
repository, found through `LANDIN_FONTS` or beside this one, and a host
without it renders in the fallback stack while the publishing workflow
refuses. The module reads each family's own stylesheet rather than
transcribing thirty `unicode-range` lists, and `check.py` holds every
character of every rendered document to falling inside a subset of both
families, because a range nobody covers is a paragraph in a fallback face
that no word count can see. Add a family there rather than in a consumer,
keep it standard-library-only, and do not trim the subsets to today's
documents. See `assets/fonts/README.md`.

Syntax highlighting lives in `highlight/`, not in the site renderer. `highlight/landin_highlight.py` owns the lexical vocabulary rendered by the pages, Pygments, TextMate, Vim, Notepad++, Nano and Kate; `highlight/tree-sitter/` is the checked structural grammar whose queries feed Neovim, Helix and Zed. Emacs provides both native and tree-sitter modes. Generate copied artifacts with `highlight/generate.py`, add a keyword at the shared source rather than in a consumer, and keep the mandatory generator path standard-library-only so the site keeps its no-dependency build. `check.py`'s own list of reserved words is deliberately separate: that one is about legality, this one about colour. See `highlight/README.md`.
